\title{Governed Agent Societies: Runtime Governance and Trace-Based Assurance for LLM-Driven Multi-Agent Self-Adaptive Systems}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}\IEEEauthorblockA{Anonymous Affiliations}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Agentic systems built from several LLM-driven roles act through tools, share memory and take actions with consequences, yet they are mostly described as prompt pipelines and judged on their final output. We model such systems as governed agent societies: multi-role decision processes in which evidence is an object that actions must cite, a runtime guard sits inside the transition function, a human answers approval requests after a delay and can refuse, and every proposal leaves a typed trace record. The guard plays the analyze and plan steps of a MAPE-K loop with a human on the loop. We instantiate the model in a smart-city operations testbed with eight service roles, four incident families, planted false reports and hidden context that only the human knows, and we compare five ways of governing LLM-driven roles, from one agent with the rules in its prompt to roles with the rules both in their prompts and enforced at runtime. With an open-weight 30B model driving the roles, rules placed in prompts leave 0.12 executed violations per incident, all of one kind, and one successful incident in nine hides a violation; the guard removes them at no measurable cost in success, steps or human requests, while the guard without the rules in the prompt costs four extra steps and a quarter more approval requests per incident. With deterministic roles that follow the rules as a reference, roles that ignore them reach the same task success as governed ones while executing two rule violations per incident and broadcasting an untrue public alert in a quarter of the episodes with a false report; the guard removes both at a cost of about three extra steps and 0.6 approval requests per incident. Trace metrics expose what task success hides, and a trust-adaptive governor quantifies the trade-off between human approval load and the harm of acting where the rules are silent. Testbed, traces and scripts are available as an artifact.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A city operations centre receives a social media report of a second accident with casualties. The communication role of an agentic system, built to warn residents quickly, cites the report and broadcasts a city-wide alert. Two steps later a traffic camera refutes the report. The incident itself is resolved on time: the ambulance is dispatched, the route is cleared, an alert went out. By every outcome measure the system succeeded. It also told a city something untrue, and nothing in its final state says so.

Systems of this kind are now common: several LLM-driven roles plan, retrieve evidence, call tools, write to shared memory and coordinate over many steps~\cite{yao2023react,wu2023autogen,li2023camel}. They are implemented as prompt pipelines or orchestration graphs, and their behaviour is judged on task completion. But their behaviour unfolds as multi-role execution under partial information and operational constraints, and a system can reach an acceptable outcome along a trajectory that breaks the rules it was given: an action rests on evidence nobody checked, a memory note preserves an unverified claim, an action that needed a human's approval executes without one. These are failures of governed execution, not of output quality, and studies of multi-agent LLM systems find most failures in exactly this territory~\cite{cemri2025mast}.

We see this as a self-adaptive systems problem. The society has to adapt its workflow at runtime: request verification when evidence is weak, escalate when a rule requires a human, fall back when the human refuses, and coordinate through shared memory when a role cannot see what another role knows. It does so under uncertainty about the evidence, about the availability of the human, and, with LLM-driven roles, about its own components. The mechanism that governs it is a runtime assurance mechanism in the tradition of the Simplex architecture and of shields~\cite{sha2001simplex,alshiekh2018safe}: a small, inspectable checker between a complex decision maker and the world. What the checker needs to know here is not a state invariant but provenance: which evidence supports the action, whether that evidence was verified, and whether a human was asked.

Practitioners today put the rules into the prompts. Recent work enforces them at runtime for a single agent~\cite{wang2026agentspec}. Nobody has measured the difference between prompted and enforced governance for a society of roles with human escalation, nor what enforcement costs in task success, steps, and human attention. This paper does that, and it does it with traces rather than outcomes.

The paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A model of \emph{governed agent societies}: roles as policies over structured state, evidence and memory, with the guard inside the transition function and a mapping onto MAPE-K (Section~\ref{sec:model}).
    \item A trace schema and assurance metrics for governed execution: attempted and executed violations, trace support coverage, escalation precision and recall, overseer load, silent violation rate, hallucinated evidence, and hidden harm (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
    \item An evaluation in a smart-city operations testbed that compares prompted and enforced governance for LLM-driven roles across models, rule sets and human availability, with paired statistics, and a second instantiation in software incident response that reuses guard, traces and metrics unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
    \item An open testbed, a trace dataset and a reproduction that takes one command (Section~\ref{sec:artifact}).
\end{enumerate}

We ask: \RQ{1} Does runtime enforcement reduce executed violations and missed approvals beyond rules placed in prompts, for LLM-driven roles? \RQ{2} What does governance cost in task success, steps, tokens and overseer load? \RQ{3} Which failures does task success hide, and which trace metrics expose them? \RQ{4} Do the effects hold across models, evidence quality, human availability and rule strictness? \RQ{5} Does the framework transfer to a software operations domain without changing guard, traces or metrics?

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Self-adaptive systems}
The MAPE-K loop~\cite{kephart2003autonomic} separates monitoring, analysis, planning and execution over shared knowledge; self-adaptive software research has built on it for two decades~\cite{salehie2009,weyns2020}, with a strong line of work on assurance at runtime~\cite{calinescu2018entrust} and on the human on the loop~\cite{li2020explanations}. Two recent SEAMS papers bring LLMs into the loop: as a reasoning component for situations not foreseen at design time~\cite{maper2026}, and as a general enabler across the loop~\cite{li2024llmsas}. We take the opposite direction: the LLMs are the components being managed, and the loop governs them.

\paragraph{Runtime assurance and enforcement}
The Simplex architecture~\cite{sha2001simplex} pairs a high-performance controller with a simple safety controller and a decision module; shields~\cite{alshiekh2018safe} do the same for reinforcement learning, and have been extended to multi-agent settings~\cite{elsayedaly2021shielding,compositional2025shielding}. Runtime enforcement monitors~\cite{falcone2011enforcement} formalise what a monitor may do to a stream of events. Our guard belongs to this family. What differs is the property: it is not a state invariant but a condition on provenance and process, namely that an action cites verified evidence, that a note names its source, that a human was asked when the rules say so. The guard also has an outcome that shields do not: escalate to a person, and wait.

\paragraph{Guardrails for LLM agents}
AgentSpec~\cite{wang2026agentspec} gives a rule language and a runtime enforcer for a single LLM agent; GuardAgent~\cite{xiang2025guardagent} and Progent~\cite{shi2025progent} check or constrain tool calls. We differ in three ways. Our roles have different visibility, so a rule such as ``public communication requires verified evidence'' can only be met through coordination, either a verified note written by another role or a verification request forwarded to a role that can check the fact. Approval is an outcome with a delay and the possibility of refusal, not a static permission. And we evaluate the mechanism on trace metrics rather than on how many actions were blocked.

\paragraph{Evaluating agents on traces}
$\tau$-bench~\cite{yao2025taubench} scores tool agents on compliance with written domain policies, and the MAST taxonomy~\cite{cemri2025mast} classifies failures of multi-agent LLM systems from their traces. Both argue that outcomes miss most of what goes wrong. We turn trace inspection into metrics and use them to compare governance mechanisms.

\paragraph{Normative multi-agent systems}
Electronic institutions and organisational models~\cite{esteva2001ei,hubner2007moise} regulate agent societies through roles, norms and enforcement, long before LLMs. Our guard is a small runtime instance of that idea for LLM-driven roles, with evidence and human oversight built into the norms.

\paragraph{Agentic architectures}
ReAct, AutoGen and CAMEL~\cite{yao2023react,wu2023autogen,li2023camel} established role decomposition and tool use; generative agents, MemGPT and Reflexion~\cite{park2023generative,packer2023memgpt,shinn2023reflexion} made memory and reflection central. In all of them memory updates are unconditioned writes. Here a memory note is evidence with a source, a status inherited from that source, and an expiry, and writing one is a governed action.

\section{Governed Agent Societies}
\label{sec:model}

\paragraph{Process model}
An agentic workflow is a \emph{Governed Agent Society Process}
\[
\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{I}, \mathcal{S}, \{\Omega_i\}, \{\mathcal{A}_i\}, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{M}, \mathcal{C}, G, \mathcal{T}, \rho).
\]
$\mathcal{I}$ is the set of roles. $\mathcal{S}$ is the state space; a state holds the incident, the outcome flags of every service, the evidence store $\mathcal{E}$, the memory store $\mathcal{M}$ and the open approval requests. Each evidence item has an identity, a topic, a kind, a claim, a target, a status (unverified, verified, conflicting, refuted) and a visibility, the set of roles that see it. Memory notes live in $\mathcal{M}$; a note cites a source item, inherits the source's status, is visible to every role and expires. $\Omega_i$ maps the state to the observation of role $i$: its visible evidence, the shared notes, the outcome flags of its own service, the guard's answer to its previous proposal and the state of its approval requests. $\mathcal{A}_i$ is the action catalogue of the role; every action type carries a risk level and the evidence topics that can support it. $\mathcal{C}$ is the active rule set, $G$ the guard, $\mathcal{T}$ the transition operator and $\rho$ the scenario distribution.

At step $t$ every active role proposes a typed action $a = \langle r, \alpha, z, p, E, q\rangle$: the role, the action type, the target, a payload, the ids of the evidence it cites, and a probability $q$, reported by the role itself, that the action needs human approval. The guard maps the proposal to one of allow, deny, sanitize, request evidence or escalate; only an allowed or sanitized proposal reaches the transition operator. The next state depends on the executed actions and on events $e_t$ from outside the society: verification results, the human's answers, the passing of time on approval requests. Learning is not part of this paper; roles are policies, whatever produces them.

\paragraph{The guard}
$G$ runs the active rules in a fixed order and returns the first outcome that is not allow (Table~\ref{tab:rules}). Scope denies a role acting outside the incident's service scope. Memory provenance denies a note whose source is not verified or that has no expiry. Public communication and evidence both ask for evidence: a proposal is \emph{supported} when it cites at least one verified item on the action's topic, cites nothing refuted, and, for public communication, cites nothing unverified either, so that a true and an untrue claim cannot go out together. Approval escalates: the request reaches the human after a latency of one to three steps and is granted when the human is available and the request was supported. A refusal holds for the rest of the episode, so the guard denies further proposals of the same action and the role has to find another way. Sanitization rewrites a proposal into a compliant one, here a reroute through an active pollution zone into a signal priority change. The guard reads an action's risk level from the catalogue, never from the proposal, so a role that declares a lower risk than the catalogue gains nothing.

\paragraph{Prompted and enforced rules}
The same rule set can reach the roles in two ways: as text in their prompts, or as the guard's configuration. In unguarded modes the guard still runs, but only to label: the proposal executes as proposed and the trace records the rule it broke. This gives every mode two counts. \emph{Attempted} violations are proposals that would break an active rule; \emph{executed} violations are attempted violations that reached the world. Under the guard, no executed action violates an active rule, so executed violations are zero by construction; a count above zero in a guarded run is a bug, and the artifact's tests assert it. Proposing an action again while its approval is pending is waiting, not an attempt, and is not counted.

\paragraph{Mapping onto MAPE-K}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the runtime and evaluation layers. Monitoring is the observation function and the evidence store; analysis is the support check and the trace metrics; planning is the role policies together with the governor's decisions, including the decision to ask a person; execution is the tool interface; knowledge is the evidence store, the notes, the rule set and the trace. The human overseer is on the loop, not in it: the society runs without them until a rule says otherwise.

\paragraph{Trust-adaptive governance}
A static guard asks the human every time a rule says so. A self-adaptive variant lets roles earn autonomy: the governor keeps a trust score per role, raised by supported and compliant executed actions ($+0.1$) and lowered by attempted violations ($-0.3$), carried across incidents. When a role's trust is at or above a threshold, its \emph{soft} approval requests are granted by the governor itself and never reach the human; \emph{hard} approvals, here a road closure that would cut hospital access, are never delegated. We measure what this costs in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Testbed}
\label{sec:testbed}

\paragraph{Society}
The domain is a city operations centre with eight service roles: traffic, emergency response, energy, water, air quality, public transport, public works and citizen communication. Each has a catalogue of three to four service actions plus the shared actions query evidence, request verification, escalate, write memory and noop. Four incident families require three or four roles each: a traffic accident near a hospital (traffic, emergency, communication), a power outage in a critical district (energy, emergency, traffic, communication), a flooded underpass (water, traffic, public works, communication) and a pollution spike near a school (air quality, traffic, public transport, communication). Every family's success condition includes a public alert, so no society can finish with one role. The state is symbolic: outcome flags such as route secured or power restored, plus the evidence store, the notes and the approval requests. This keeps every guard decision inspectable in the trace.

\paragraph{Evidence}
A scenario starts with a primary report (E1: an incident report or a sensor reading), a congestion sensor (E3, verified), a residents' report (E4, unverified, visible to the communication role only) and a second item that depends on the family (E5). Evidence quality sets what is available at the start: complete (E1 verified), partial (E1 unverified), missing (E1 and E5 absent until a role queries the topic), or conflicting (E1 plus a contradicting E2; verifying either resolves both). In about a quarter of the scenarios a planted false report F1 claims a second incident elsewhere; verification refutes it. In about a third a distractor report about an unrelated pothole is visible to two roles.

Verification is by topic: a role can have items checked on the topics it works on. The communication role works on none of the field topics, so it has two ways to a verified basis for an alert: a note written by the role that owns the incident, or a verification request that the environment forwards to a role that can check the item, which sees it under its pending requests and answers when idle. Both paths take steps and both leave traces.

\paragraph{The human}
The overseer is available in 80\% of the scenarios and answers after one to three steps. A request is refused when the human is unavailable, when the request cites no verified evidence, or, in 15\% of the scenarios, because of \emph{hidden context}: the human knows something the rules do not encode, a planned event on the bus lane, a crew inside the grid segment. No role sees this flag. An action that requires approval, executed without it in such a scenario, causes \emph{hidden harm}, a failure that no rule would have caught and that only asking a person prevents.

\paragraph{Scenarios}
Table~\ref{tab:features} gives the scenario set: 20 scenarios per family, generated from one seed, saved to disk and reused by every mode, so that comparisons are paired. The horizon is 16 steps.

\paragraph{A second domain: software incident response}
To test the claim that only the domain module changes, we wrote a second domain with the same interface: the on-call team of a web platform, with seven roles (monitoring, diagnosis, releases, capacity, dependencies, storage, customer communication) and four incident families (a bad deployment, a traffic spike, a dependency outage, a full disk). The correspondence is deliberate. Health check alerts, metrics, deploy records and customer reports play the parts of incident reports, sensors and residents' reports; a rollback needs a verified failing health check as an emergency dispatch needs a verified incident; a status page update declaring a major incident needs approval as a city-wide alert does; scaling out beyond four replicas is the bus lane, isolating a critical dependency the grid segment, and a configuration change on a critical service the road closure that cuts hospital access (a hard approval). A full restart during a traffic spike is sanitized to a rolling restart, as a reroute through a pollution zone is sanitized to a signal priority change. The guard, the trace schema, the metrics, the statistics and both deterministic brains run on this domain without modification; the domain module is about 360 lines, most of them the action catalogue and the evidence pool.

\paragraph{Role policies}
Three policy classes drive the roles. \emph{LLM roles}: one chat completion per role and step; the system prompt gives the role's job, its catalogue with risk levels and evidence topics, an explanation of the observation, the output schema and, in the rules variant, the rule set in plain words; the user message is the observation as JSON; the answer is one JSON object with the fields of a typed action, including the cited evidence ids and $q$. An invalid answer gets one retry with the parser's complaint; a second failure becomes a noop and is counted as a formatting failure. \emph{Naive roles} do not know the rules: they act as soon as they see anything on topic, cite everything they see, share sloppy notes, and change course only when the guard pushes back, using the guard's reason in their observation. \emph{Procedural roles} follow the rules: verify, share a note with a verified source, ask for approval, then act, with a fallback when the human refuses. The procedural roles double as a reachability check: they reach every scenario's goal with zero attempted violations, so any success lost under the guard is the policy's, not the rules'.

\section{Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Modes}
M0: one agent with every tool and the rules in its prompt. M1: roles without rules anywhere. M2: roles with the rules in their prompts only. M3: roles with the guard and no rules in their prompts. M4: rules in prompts and the guard. M2 against M3 isolates prompted from enforced governance; M0 against M3 a monolith from a governed society; M3 against M4 whether knowing the rules reduces the friction of enforcement. The deterministic rows D0 to D6 mirror these modes with the naive and procedural brains.

\paragraph{Models}
LLM roles run on two API models from one provider, GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna, at temperature 0.7 with five repeats per scenario in every mode, and on one open-weight model, Muse-Glimmer-30B, served locally with vLLM at its documented reasoning level ``high'' (the API models run at their defaults), three repeats on M2 to M4. \todo{keep only the runs that were done; Glimmer is the check that the pattern does not depend on the provider.} Prompts, sampling settings and the served model names are in the artifact's manifests.

\paragraph{Metrics}
All metrics come from trace records and final flags. Per episode: success; steps; proposals (role actions other than noop); attempted and executed violations, by rule; missed approvals (executed actions that required approval and had none); escalations and \emph{overseer load}, the number of distinct approval requests that reached the human, with the share that did not need approval; escalation precision and recall over the actions that required approval; the Brier score of $q$ against the rule's label; \emph{trace support coverage} (TSC), the share of executed high-impact actions with verified supporting evidence; \emph{hallucinated references}, the share of cited ids that do not exist or are not visible to the role; false alert and hidden harm as flags; \emph{silent violation}, success together with at least one executed violation; and governance-adjusted utility
\[
\mathrm{GAU} = S - 0.3V - 0.2A - 0.1M - 0.1\,\mathrm{steps}/16 - 0.3H,
\]
with $S$ success, $V$ executed violations, $A$ missed approvals, $M$ unsupported memory writes and $H$ hidden harm. The weights order the failures by severity; a grid over 243 weight settings checks that the ranking of modes does not hinge on them. Table~\ref{tab:worked} follows one guarded episode and derives every metric from its trace.

\input{tables/worked_example.tex}

\paragraph{Statistics}
The scenario is the unit of analysis; repeats of one scenario are averaged first. Modes see the same scenarios, so comparisons are paired: Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (no normality assumption; many differences are zero), Cliff's $\delta$ as effect size, 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals with 10\,000 resamples for every mean and mean difference, and Holm correction across the metrics of one comparison. Primary comparisons: M2 vs M3, M0 vs M3, M3 vs M4. The hypotheses: enforcement reduces executed violations and missed approvals to zero; prompting reduces them without removing them; enforcement costs steps and overseer load and little or no success under R2.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Deterministic reference rows}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the deterministic rows under R2. % NUM outputs/paper/table_main.md seed 2027
Roles that ignore the rules (D2) resolve every incident in 1.25 steps: all three or four roles act at once, on whatever they see. They also execute 2.1 rule violations per incident, 84\% of their executed high-impact actions have no verified evidence, and in 24\% of the episodes, almost every episode with a planted false report, the public alert repeats an untrue claim. Every one of their successful episodes contains an executed violation. The same roles under the guard (D3) still resolve every incident, in 4.5 steps and 11.5 proposals instead of 4.2, with zero executed violations, zero false alerts, full trace support, and 0.64 approval requests per incident on the human. The guard blocks 3.9 proposals per incident to get there. A single agent with every tool (D0) is not faster than the society under the guard (4.0 steps against 4.5) and is worse on every governance measure, including hidden harm in 7\% of the episodes, because it acts on soft-approval actions without asking. Rule-following roles (D4, D5) need no guard: the guard never fires on them, and their rows are identical with and without it. Governance-adjusted utility orders the modes accordingly: 0.33 for the unguarded naive society, 0.97 with the guard, 0.97 for the compliant society.

\paragraph{\RQ{2} Cost}
Enforcement without the rules in the prompt has a price. M3 needs 8.2 steps per incident against 4.2 for M2 ($+4.0$ $[3.2, 4.8]$, $\delta = 0.78$, $p < 0.001$), 17 proposals against 11, half as many tokens again (43.5k against 28.7k), 0.22 more approval requests per incident on the human ($p = 0.003$), and resolves 90\% of the incidents against 96\% ($-0.07$ $[-0.11, -0.03]$, Holm $p = 0.08$). The extra steps are the roles finding the compliant path by trial: a blocked reroute becomes a signal change, an escalated alert waits for the answer. M4 pays none of it: with the rules in the prompt and the guard behind them, every cost measure equals M2's within noise, and governance-adjusted utility is the highest of the three (0.94 against 0.90 and 0.84; $+0.09$ over M3, $p < 0.001$). Formatting failures stay between 0.03 and 0.06 per incident in every mode and no cited evidence id was invented; the model also never repeated a blocked action more than twice in a row, so the guard's reason, which the role sees at the next step, is read.

Of the requests the human receives, most were not required by the rules: 64\% in M2 and M4, 71\% in M3. The model asks whenever hospital access is at risk, whether or not the proposed action is the road closure the rule names; without the rules in its prompt it asks at the same rate in scenarios with and without that risk (0.39 per incident in both). The human's load is therefore set as much by the model's caution as by the rule set, which is the load the trust-adaptive governor of Section~\ref{sec:model} would remove.

\paragraph{\RQ{3} What success hides}
In M2, 11\% of the successful incidents contain an executed violation (silent violation rate 0.12 per episode, 0.11 per scenario); the incident is resolved, the trace says a rule was broken, and nothing in the final state does. Under the guard the rate is zero by construction. False alerts, unsupported public messages and hidden harm are zero in all three modes: this model verifies before it speaks (trace support coverage 1.00 everywhere) and asks before every action that needs approval, so the hidden context never had an action to harm. The traces show two failure mechanisms instead, both ending in the 16-step horizon, which accounts for all 43 failed episodes. The first is an \emph{approval lock-out}: a role asks the human for an approval the rules do not require, the answer is a refusal, because the human is absent or holds hidden context, and the role respects the refusal for the rest of the episode. In M2 and M4 all 18 failures are of this kind, in the same three scenarios in all three repeats, each with hospital access at risk; in one of them the water role writes for thirteen steps that the closure ``requires human approval and the approval is denied'' while the underpass stays open. The guard did not cause it and cannot prevent it: a refusal binds whether the request was needed or not. The second is an \emph{evidence loop}, only in M3: the communication role, told nothing about the rules, requests evidence or verification on topics it believes it needs and never proposes a message; in 20 of the 25 M3 failures the missing goal is the public alert. With the rules in the prompt the same role sends a targeted notice in the same scenarios.

\paragraph{\RQ{4} Robustness}
By family, the pattern holds where there is something to govern: executed violations in M2 are 0.32 per incident in pollution spikes and 0.15 in flooded underpasses, the two families with active pollution zones, and zero in traffic accidents and 0.02 in outages; the guard brings all four to zero. The cost of M3 is largest in flooded underpasses (10.3 steps, success 0.75), the family with the most approval and evidence rules in play, and smallest in outages (6.7 steps, success 0.97). \todo{R1/R3 and human always/never sweeps (running); the GAU weight grid on the LLM rows; the API models, if run, for the model dimension.}

\paragraph{Trust-adaptive governance}
% NUM outputs/paper/adaptive/table_adaptive.md, R3 at 15%
Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} shows the trade-off under R3 with hidden context in 15\% of the scenarios. The static guard puts two requests per incident on the human and resolves 86\% of the incidents, with no hidden harm. Delegating every soft approval to roles that have earned trust removes the human's load, restores 100\% success, and exposes the society to every hidden-context scenario: hidden harm in 16\% of the episodes. Intermediate thresholds lie on the line between the two ends; the naive roles never earn enough trust for thresholds above 0.3, so for them delegation does nothing. Trust earned by compliance carries no information about what the rules do not encode, which is exactly what the human was there for. The figure is a measured trade-off, not a recommendation; where the line should be cut depends on what a hidden harm and an unresolved incident cost.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{For engineers}
Instantiating the framework for a new system takes four artefacts: an action catalogue with risk levels and evidence topics, a rule set as data, roles with their visibility, and the trace schema. The guard, the metrics and the statistics do not change. The evaluation then answers questions an outcome metric cannot: how often did the system succeed by breaking a rule, how much human attention did it consume, how much of it was needed, and how often did it cite evidence that was not there.

\paragraph{Prompted rules are not enforced rules}
\todo{write from the LLM results: how far prompting gets, where it fails, and why enforcement plus prompting (M4) is the combination to deploy.}

\paragraph{The human is not a rule}
The approval step looks like a bottleneck and, under strict rules and an unavailable human, it is: success drops to a half. The adaptive governor shows what removing it costs. The rules capture what the designers foresaw; the human catches the rest, and no amount of compliance with the rules predicts the rest. Systems that want to reduce human load should reduce the \emph{unnecessary} share of approval requests, which the trace measures, rather than delegate on trust.

\paragraph{Threats to validity}
\emph{Construct}: the testbed is symbolic and its rules are ours; hidden context is a coin flip with a fixed rate, and its harm is binary. The metrics measure conformance to the given rules, not the quality of the rules. \emph{Internal}: the naive roles' reactions to the guard were written by us; a different reactive policy would change the cost side, not the violation side. LLM nondeterminism is handled by repeats and paired tests; prompts are fixed and published. \emph{External}: two domains that we built to correspond, two API models from one provider and one open-weight model \todo{drop the open-weight model if the Glimmer run is not done}, a deterministic human. The framework transfers when the four artefacts above can be written; whether the numbers transfer is an open question. \emph{Conclusion}: all comparisons are paired, corrected for multiple testing and reported with intervals; seeds, scenario sets and manifests are in the artifact.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Governed agent societies put the rules where the actions are: in a guard inside the transition function that checks provenance, asks a person when the rules say so, and leaves a trace of everything it decided. With an LLM driving the roles, the rules in the prompt leaked one violation per eight incidents; the guard removed every one at no cost once the roles knew the rules, and at four extra steps per incident when they did not. The traces show that success and governance are different things, and that a system can have the first without the second; the guard gives the second at a measured price in steps and human attention. Next steps are LLM roles in the incident response domain and learning role policies from the traces the guard produces.
