\title{Governed Agent Societies: Runtime Governance and Trace-Based Assurance for LLM-Driven Multi-Agent Self-Adaptive Systems}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}\IEEEauthorblockA{Anonymous Affiliations}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Agentic systems built from several LLM-driven roles act through tools, share memory and take actions with consequences, yet they are mostly described as prompt pipelines and judged on their final output. This paper models such systems as governed agent societies: multi-role decision processes in which evidence is an object that actions must cite, a runtime guard sits inside the transition function, a human answers approval requests after a delay and can refuse, and every proposal leaves a typed trace record. The roles are the managed system; the guard, the human and the trace metrics are the managing system of a MAPE-K loop with a human on the loop. The model is instantiated in a smart-city operations testbed (eight roles, four incident families, planted false reports, hidden context only the human knows), and five ways of governing LLM-driven roles are compared, from one agent with the rules in its prompt to roles with the rules in their prompts and enforced at runtime. With four open-weight models of 4B to 122B parameters, rules in prompts leave between 0.12 and 0.96 executed violations per incident, and up to 51\% of the successful incidents hide a violation. For the three reasoning models nearly every leak is one rule. The model that answers directly also acts without approval, or before it arrives, in more than half of its violations, while its own rationale says approval is required; a reasoning model with its reasoning turned off does the same and leaks twice as much. A prompt that repeats the rules at every step removes a quarter to a half of the leak. The guard removes all of it: at no measurable cost for the reasoning models, and at 10 to 14 points of success for the models that answer directly, the approvals their prompted roles had skipped. Without the rules in the prompts, enforcement costs the reasoning models four extra steps per incident or twice the human's approval requests, depending on the model. A trust-adaptive governor quantifies the trade-off between human approval load and the harm of acting where the rules are silent, and a second domain, software incident response, reuses guard, traces and metrics unchanged and shows the same leak and the same zero for two models. Testbed, traces and scripts are available as an artifact.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A city operations centre receives a social media report of a second accident with casualties. The communication role of an agentic system, built to warn residents quickly, cites the report and broadcasts a city-wide alert. Two steps later a traffic camera refutes the report. The incident itself is resolved on time: the ambulance is dispatched, the route is cleared, an alert went out. By every outcome measure the system succeeded. It also told a city something untrue, and nothing in its final state says so.

Systems of this kind are now common: several LLM-driven roles plan, retrieve evidence, call tools, write to shared memory and coordinate over many steps~\cite{yao2023react,wu2023autogen,li2023camel}. But a system can reach an acceptable outcome along a trajectory that breaks the rules it was given: an action rests on evidence nobody checked, a memory note preserves an unverified claim, an action that needed a human's approval executes without one. These are failures of governed execution, not of output quality, and a taxonomy of multi-agent LLM failures finds many of them in specification and coordination~\cite{cemri2025mast}. Which of them a model commits is an empirical question: here no LLM repeated the planted false report; the leaks were elsewhere, in a rule tied to a state flag and in approvals.

This is a self-adaptive systems problem. At runtime the society must request verification when evidence is weak, escalate when a rule requires a human, fall back when the human refuses, and share what one role sees and another does not. It does so under uncertainty about the evidence, the human and, with LLM-driven roles, its own components. The mechanism that governs it is runtime assurance in the tradition of the Simplex architecture and of shields~\cite{sha2001simplex,alshiekh2018safe}: a small, inspectable checker between a complex decision maker and the world. What the checker needs to know here is not a state invariant but provenance: which evidence supports the action, whether it was verified, and whether a human was asked.

Practitioners today put the rules into the prompts. Recent work enforces them at runtime for a single agent~\cite{wang2026agentspec}. No published measurement separates prompted from enforced governance for a society of roles with a human on the loop, or says what enforcement costs in success, steps, tokens and human attention. This paper makes that measurement, on traces rather than outcomes.

The paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A model of \emph{governed agent societies}: roles as policies over structured state, evidence and memory, with the guard inside the transition function and a mapping onto MAPE-K (Section~\ref{sec:model}).
    \item A trace schema and assurance metrics for governed execution: attempted and executed violations, trace support coverage, overseer load and its unnecessary share, silent violation rate, hallucinated evidence, and hidden harm (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
    \item An evaluation in a smart-city operations testbed that compares prompted and enforced governance for LLM-driven roles across four open-weight models of 4B to 122B parameters\todo{API: ``and two API models''}, with controls for reasoning and prompt wording, rule sets and human availability, with paired statistics, and a second instantiation in software incident response that reuses guard, traces and metrics unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
    \item An open testbed, a trace dataset and a reproduction of every table in one command (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
\end{enumerate}

Five questions: \RQ{1} How many executed violations and missed approvals do rules placed in prompts leave for LLM-driven roles, and does enforcing the same rules at runtime remove them? \RQ{2} What does governance cost in task success, steps, tokens and overseer load? \RQ{3} Which failures does task success hide, and which trace metrics expose them? \RQ{4} Do the effects hold across models, with and without reasoning, under a stronger prompt, and across incident families, evidence quality, human availability and rule strictness? \RQ{5} Does the framework transfer to a software operations domain without changing guard, traces or metrics?

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Self-adaptive systems}
The MAPE-K loop~\cite{kephart2003autonomic} separates monitoring, analysis, planning and execution over shared knowledge; self-adaptive software research has built on it for two decades~\cite{salehie2009,weyns2020}, including decentralized control over several components~\cite{weyns2013patterns}, assurance at runtime~\cite{calinescu2018entrust} and human participation~\cite{camara2015human,li2020explanations}. Two recent SEAMS papers bring LLMs into the loop, as a reasoning component for unforeseen situations~\cite{maper2026} and as an enabler across the loop~\cite{li2024llmsas}; this paper takes the opposite direction: the LLMs are the components being managed, and the loop governs them.

\paragraph{Runtime assurance and enforcement}
The Simplex architecture~\cite{sha2001simplex} pairs a high-performance controller with a simple safety controller and a decision module; shields~\cite{alshiekh2018safe} do the same for reinforcement learning, also in multi-agent settings~\cite{elsayedaly2021shielding,compositional2025shielding}; runtime enforcement monitors~\cite{falcone2011enforcement} formalise what a monitor may do to a stream of events. The guard here belongs to this family. What differs is the property, not a state invariant but a condition on provenance and process: that an action cites verified evidence, that a note names its source, that a human was asked when the rules say so. The guard also has an outcome that shields do not: escalate to a person, and wait.

\paragraph{Guardrails for LLM agents}
Programmable rails~\cite{rebedea2023nemo} and agent constitutions~\cite{hua2024trustagent} put the rules into the conversation; AgentSpec~\cite{wang2026agentspec} gives a rule language and a runtime enforcer for a single LLM agent; GuardAgent~\cite{xiang2025guardagent}, Progent~\cite{shi2025progent} and ShieldAgent~\cite{chen2025shieldagent} check or constrain tool calls. Three things differ here. The roles have different visibility, so a rule such as ``public communication requires verified evidence'' can only be met through coordination (a verified note from another role, or a verification request forwarded to one that can check). Approval is an outcome with a delay and the possibility of refusal, not a static permission. The mechanism is evaluated on trace metrics rather than on how many actions were blocked.

\paragraph{Evaluating agents on traces}
$\tau$-bench~\cite{yao2025taubench} scores tool agents on compliance with written domain policies, and the MAST taxonomy~\cite{cemri2025mast} classifies failures of multi-agent LLM systems from their traces; both argue that outcomes miss most of what goes wrong. Sandboxes such as ToolEmu~\cite{ruan2024toolemu} and AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo} measure a single agent's unsafe tool calls under emulated risk or prompt injection. Conformance checking~\cite{carmona2018conformance} replays event logs against a process model. Here trace inspection becomes metrics for several roles, where the risk is procedural (provenance, approval), and the metrics compare governance mechanisms.

\paragraph{Agent societies and architectures}
Electronic institutions and organisational models~\cite{esteva2001ei,hubner2007moise} regulated agent societies through roles, norms and enforcement long before LLMs, with norm monitoring~\cite{dastani2018monitoring} and programming platforms~\cite{boissier2020jacamo} of their own; the guard here is a small runtime instance of that idea for LLM-driven roles, with evidence and human oversight built into the norms. ReAct, AutoGen and CAMEL~\cite{yao2023react,wu2023autogen,li2023camel} established role decomposition and tool use; generative agents, MemGPT and Reflexion~\cite{park2023generative,packer2023memgpt,shinn2023reflexion} made memory central, as unconditioned writes; here a note is evidence with a source, an inherited status and an expiry, and writing one is a governed action.

\section{Governed Agent Societies}
\label{sec:model}

\paragraph{Process model}
An agentic workflow is a \emph{Governed Agent Society Process}
\[
\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{I}, \mathcal{S}, \{\Omega_i\}, \{\mathcal{A}_i\}, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{M}, \mathcal{C}, G, \mathcal{T}, \rho).
\]
$\mathcal{I}$ is the set of roles. $\mathcal{S}$ is the state space; a state consists of the incident, the outcome flags of every service, the evidence store $\mathcal{E}$, the memory store $\mathcal{M}$ and the open approval requests. An evidence item has an identity, a topic, a claim, a target, a status (unverified, verified, conflicting, refuted) and a visibility, the roles that see it; a memory note cites a source item, inherits its status, is visible to every role and expires. $\Omega_i$ maps the state to the observation of role $i$: its visible evidence, the shared notes, the outcome flags of its own service, the guard's answer to its previous proposal and the state of its approval requests. $\mathcal{A}_i$ is the action catalogue of the role; every action type carries a risk level, the evidence topics that can support it, and whether it is \emph{high-impact}, acting on the world rather than on information. $\mathcal{C}$ is the active rule set, $G$ the guard, $\mathcal{T}$ the transition operator and $\rho$ the scenario distribution.

At step $t$ every active role proposes a typed action $a = \langle r, \alpha, z, p, E, q\rangle$: the role, the action type, the target, a payload, the ids of the evidence it cites, and a probability $q$, reported by the role itself, that the action needs human approval. The guard maps the proposal to one of allow, deny, sanitize, request evidence or escalate; only an allowed or sanitized proposal reaches the transition operator. The next state depends on the executed actions and on events $e_t$ from outside the society: verification results, the human's answers, the passing of time on approval requests.

\paragraph{The guard}
$G$ runs the active rules in a fixed order and returns the first outcome that is not allow (Table~\ref{tab:rules}). Scope denies a role acting outside the incident's service scope. Memory provenance denies a note whose source is not verified or that has no expiry. Public communication and evidence both ask for evidence: a proposal is \emph{supported} when it cites at least one verified item on the action's topic, cites nothing refuted, and, for public communication, cites nothing unverified either, so that a true and an untrue claim cannot go out together. Approval escalates: the request reaches the human after a latency of one to three steps and is granted when the human is available and the request was supported. A refusal stays in force for the rest of the episode, so the guard denies further proposals of the same action and the role has to find another way. Sanitization rewrites a proposal into a compliant one, here a reroute through an active pollution zone into a signal priority change. Risk levels come from the catalogue, not from the proposal.

\paragraph{Prompted and enforced rules}
The same rule set can reach the roles in two ways: as text in their prompts, or as the guard's configuration. In unguarded modes the guard still runs, but only to label: the proposal executes as proposed and the trace records the rule it broke. This gives every mode two counts. \emph{Attempted} violations are proposals that would break an active rule; \emph{executed} violations are attempted violations that reached the world. Under the guard no executed action violates an active rule. This is the guard's guarantee: in guarded runs executed violations, missed approvals, silent violations, false alerts and hidden harm are zero by construction (the artifact's tests assert it) and only success, steps, attempts, requests, tokens and formatting can vary. It covers neither rules outside $\mathcal{C}$, nor what only the human knows, nor task success, and its price is liveness: a blocked or waiting role may never reach its goal. A proposal that breaks several rules counts once, under the first rule in the guard's order; proposing an action again while its approval is pending is waiting, not an attempt, and is not counted.

\paragraph{Mapping onto MAPE-K}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the runtime and evaluation layers. The society of roles is the managed system; the guard, the human and the trace metrics are the managing system. Monitoring is the observation function and the evidence store; analysis is the support check and the trace metrics; planning is the guard's decision on each proposal, including the decision to ask a person; execution is the tool interface; knowledge is the evidence store, the notes, the rule set and the trace. The human overseer is on the loop, not in it: the society runs without them until a rule says otherwise.

\paragraph{Trust-adaptive governance}
A static guard asks the human every time a rule says so. A self-adaptive variant, the trust-adaptive governor, lets roles earn autonomy: the governor keeps a trust score per role in $[0, 1]$, starting at 0.4, raised by supported and compliant executed actions ($+0.1$) and lowered by attempted violations ($-0.3$), carried across incidents. When a role's trust is at or above a threshold, its \emph{soft} approval requests are granted by the governor itself and never reach the human; \emph{hard} approvals, here a road closure that would cut hospital access, are never delegated. Section~\ref{sec:results} measures what this costs.

\section{Testbed}
\label{sec:testbed}

\paragraph{Society}
The domain is a city operations centre with eight service roles: traffic, emergency response, energy, water, air quality, public transport, public works and citizen communication. Each has a catalogue of three to four service actions plus the shared actions query evidence, request verification, escalate, write memory and noop. Four incident families require three or four roles each: a traffic accident near a hospital (traffic, emergency, communication), a power outage in a critical district (energy, emergency, traffic, communication), a flooded underpass (water, traffic, public works, communication) and a pollution spike near a school (air quality, traffic, public transport, communication). A family's success condition is its remediation flags (route secured and ambulance dispatched, power restored, underpass closed and crew sent, traffic restricted) plus a public alert, so no single service role can resolve an incident alone. The state is symbolic, which keeps every guard decision inspectable in the trace.

\paragraph{Evidence}
A scenario starts with a primary report (E1: an incident report or a sensor reading), a congestion sensor (E3, verified), a residents' report (E4, unverified, visible to the communication role only) and an item specific to the family (E5). Evidence quality sets what is available at the start: complete (E1 verified), partial (E1 unverified), missing (E1 and E5 absent until a role queries the topic) or conflicting (E1 plus a contradicting E2; verifying either resolves both). In 19 of the 80 scenarios a planted false report F1 claims a second incident elsewhere, which verification refutes; in 14 a distractor report about an unrelated pothole is visible to two roles.

Verification is by topic: a role can have items checked on the topics it works on. The communication role works on none of the field topics, so an alert needs either a note from the role that owns the incident or a verification request forwarded to a role that can check the item, which has to take it up.

\paragraph{The human}
The human overseer is available in 66 of the 80 scenarios and answers after one to three steps. A request is refused when the human is unavailable (absence counts as refusal), when the request cites no verified evidence, or, in 13 scenarios, because of \emph{hidden context}: the human knows something the rules do not encode, a planned event on the bus lane, a crew inside the grid segment. No role sees this flag. An action that requires approval, executed without it in such a scenario, causes \emph{hidden harm}, a failure that no rule would have caught and that only asking a person prevents.

\paragraph{A second domain: software incident response}
To test the claim that only the domain module changes, a second domain was written with the same interface: the on-call team of a web platform, with seven roles (monitoring, diagnosis, releases, capacity, dependencies, storage, customer communication) and four incident families (a bad deployment, a traffic spike, a dependency outage, a full disk). Each city rule has a counterpart: a rollback needs a verified failing health check as an emergency dispatch needs a verified incident; a status page update declaring a major incident needs approval as a city-wide alert does; scaling out beyond four replicas is the bus lane, isolating a critical dependency the grid segment, a configuration change on a critical service the hard approval of the hospital road; a full restart during a traffic spike is sanitized to a rolling restart as the reroute through a pollution zone is to a signal change. The guard, the trace schema, the metrics, the statistics and both deterministic role policies run on this domain without modification; the domain module is about 360 lines.

\paragraph{Role policies}
Three policy classes drive the roles. \emph{LLM roles}: one chat completion per role and step; the system prompt gives the role's job, its catalogue with risk levels and evidence topics, the observation format, the output schema and, in the rules variant, the rule set in plain words; the user message is the observation as JSON; the answer is one JSON object with the fields of a typed action, the cited evidence ids and $q$. An invalid answer gets one retry with the parser's complaint; a second failure becomes a noop and counts as a formatting failure. \emph{Naive roles} do not know the rules: they act as soon as they see anything on topic, cite everything they see, share sloppy notes, and change course only when the guard pushes back. \emph{Procedural roles} follow the rules: verify, share a sourced note, ask for approval, act, fall back when the human refuses. The procedural roles double as a reachability check: they reach every goal with no attempted violation, so when a policy loses success under the guard, the policy is the cause, not the rules.

\section{Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Modes}
M0: one agent with every tool and the rules in its prompt. M1: roles without rules anywhere. M2: roles with the rules in their prompts only. M3: roles with the guard and no rules in their prompts. M4: rules in prompts and the guard. M1 to M4 cover the four combinations of rules in the prompts and the guard; M0 against M3 compares a monolith with a governed society. The deterministic rows mirror them with the two policies of Section~\ref{sec:testbed}: D2 and D3 are M1 and M3 with naive roles, D4 and D5 are M2 and M4 with roles that follow the rules, D0 and D6 the single agent with each policy.

\paragraph{Models}
LLM roles run on four open-weight models served locally with vLLM. Three reason before they answer (level ``high'' where the model has levels): Gemma 4 E4B, a small model with about 4B effective parameters; Muse-Glimmer-30B (Glimmer below), a dense 30B model; and gpt-oss-120b, a mixture of experts with 117B parameters of which about 5B are active per token; temperature 0.7, three repeats per scenario and mode. The fourth, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B, a mixture of experts with 122B parameters and about 10B active, served in 8-bit precision, ran with its reasoning mode off and answered directly, with ten repeats per scenario and mode. Two controls separate what this model shows from reasoning and from the prompt's wording. Gemma 4 E4B ran again with its reasoning off (M2 to M4, three repeats). A \emph{reinforced} prompt for Gemma 4 E4B and Qwen3.5-122B (M2 only, three repeats) adds an emphatic paragraph after the rules (ask first and wait; never propose a forbidden action; check the rules before answering) and repeats the rules in every step's message. Glimmer and gpt-oss-120b also ran in the second domain (M2 to M4, three repeats).\todo{API: ``, and on two API models from one provider, GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna, at their default settings, temperature 0.7, five repeats per scenario and mode'' (from configs/llm\_grid.yaml and the manifests in outputs/llm/M*/)}. 

\paragraph{Metrics}
All metrics come from trace records and final flags. Per episode: success; steps; proposals (actions other than noop); attempted and executed violations, by rule; missed approvals (executed actions that required approval and had none); escalations and \emph{overseer load}, the number of distinct approval requests that reached the human, with the share that did not need approval; \emph{trace support coverage} (TSC), the share of executed high-impact actions with verified supporting evidence; \emph{hallucinated references}, the share of cited ids that do not exist or are not visible to the role; false alert and hidden harm as flags; tokens, prompt and completion including reasoning; \emph{silent violation}, success together with at least one executed violation; and governance-adjusted utility
\[
\mathrm{GAU} = S - 0.3V - 0.2A - 0.1M - 0.1\,\mathrm{steps}/16 - 0.3H,
\]
with $S$ success, $V$ executed violations, $A$ missed approvals, $M$ unsupported memory writes and $H$ hidden harm. The weights order the failures by severity and are a declared default, not a verdict; a grid of 243 settings (three values per weight) checks that the ranking of modes does not hinge on them. Table~\ref{tab:worked} derives the metrics of one guarded episode from its trace.

\paragraph{Statistics}
The scenario is the unit of analysis; repeats of one scenario are averaged first (an episode is one run of one scenario, and ``per incident'' in the text means per episode). Modes see the same scenarios, so comparisons are paired: Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Cliff's $\delta$ as effect size, 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals with 10\,000 resamples for every mean and mean difference, and Holm correction across the 14 metrics of one comparison; a difference is called a cost or a gain only when its corrected $p$ is below 0.05. Comparisons: M2 vs M3, M2 vs M4, M3 vs M4 (Table~\ref{tab:llmstats}), M0 vs M3, M1 vs M2 and M1 vs M3 (text). The hypotheses tested: prompting reduces executed violations and missed approvals without removing them; enforcement removes them, by construction, at no cost when the roles know the rules and at a cost in steps or overseer load when they do not.

\paragraph{Strict rules and an absent human}

\paragraph{\RQ{1} Enforcement against prompting}
% NUM outputs/llm/stats/paired_stats.md, outputs/llm/failure_catalogue.md (R2, 80 scenarios x 3 repeats per model)
Table~\ref{tab:llm} gives the LLM results, Table~\ref{tab:llmstats} the paired comparisons and Table~\ref{tab:ruleleak} the leak rule by rule. With the rules in their prompts and no guard (M2), the roles still execute 0.12 (Glimmer), 0.19 (gpt-oss-120b), 0.96 (Gemma 4 E4B) and 0.85 (Qwen3.5-122B) violations per incident. For the three reasoning models nearly all of them break one rule, a traffic reroute through an active pollution zone without emergency priority (every one of Glimmer's 29 and gpt-oss-120b's 46, and 212 of Gemma's 230); the model that answers directly leaks through two, 0.33 reroutes through the pollution zone and 0.49 actions per incident that needed the human's approval and had none. With the guard added (M4) executed violations are zero by construction; the reduction from M2 is significant for every model (Holm $p \leq 0.003$). For the three reasoning models success, steps, approval requests and tokens do not change ($p = 1$ for each measure); for Qwen3.5-122B the guard costs 14 points of success ($-0.14$ $[-0.21, -0.08]$, $p = 0.005$) and 1.7 steps, for a reason \RQ{2} returns to. The guard alone (M3), with roles that do not know the rules, also executes zero violations but has more to stop, 0.58, 0.80, 1.57 and 1.44 attempts per incident; knowing the rules removes most of that work, with attempts falling to 0.06, 0.22, 0.90 and 0.72 in M4 ($p < 0.001$ for each model).

For Glimmer the trace shows what the guard did. In M4 it sanitised the 15 reroutes the model still proposed and nothing else; the model kept the other five rules of R2 in every one of 240 incidents. The approval rule separates the models (Table~\ref{tab:ruleleak}): no reasoning model executed an action that needed approval in M2 except Gemma 4 E4B, eight times in 240 incidents; Qwen3.5-122B did so 389 times in 800: 217 while its own request was still pending, 153 without a request, 130 of them the road closure that cuts hospital access, and 19 after a refusal. In 106 of those 130 closures the model's rationale says that the closure needs human approval, and in 93 its reported probability $q$ is 1.0; its prompt names the action that asks (``use the escalate action to request it'') and shows the pending answer in every observation. It marks the action as needing approval and proposes it anyway, and without a guard nothing turns the mark into a request.

With no rules anywhere (M1) the same roles execute 0.53, 0.74, 1.34 and 1.35 violations per incident, and the models differ in what they do unprompted. Glimmer verifies and asks on its own (114 of its 126 violations are reroutes through the pollution zone); gpt-oss-120b and Qwen3.5-122B do not ask (0.39 and 0.70 missed approvals per incident, hidden harm in 5\% and 7\% of the incidents). \paragraph{\RQ{2} Cost}
Enforcement without the rules in the prompt has a price, paid in different currencies. Glimmer pays in steps and tokens: M3 needs 8.2 steps per incident against 4.2 for M2 ($+4.0$ $[3.2, 4.8]$, $\delta = 0.78$, $p < 0.001$), half as many tokens again (43.5k against 28.7k), 0.22 more approval requests per incident ($p = 0.003$), and resolves 90\% of the incidents against 96\% ($-0.07$ $[-0.11, -0.03]$, Holm $p = 0.08$). gpt-oss-120b and Gemma 4 E4B pay in human attention: their roles do not ask, so the guard escalates every action that needs approval, and the human receives 0.97 instead of 0.54 and 2.27 instead of 0.98 requests per incident ($p < 0.001$ for both), at little cost in steps and no significant loss of success. For these three models M4 pays nothing: with the rules in the prompt and the guard behind them, every cost measure equals M2's within noise, and governance-adjusted utility is highest in M4. Formatting failures stay at or below 0.06 per incident, and no role repeated a blocked proposal at its next step: the guard's reason is read.

Qwen3.5-122B breaks the cost half of the hypothesis, and the trace says why. In M2 it resolved 97\% of the incidents by not waiting: 217 of its approval violations executed while the request was pending and 153 without a request. Under the guard those actions wait for the human's answer or are refused, and the roles do not find the compliant alternative: all 135 failures of M4 lack the public alert, 79 of them with the alert's approval refused and no fallback to a targeted notice, the rest in verification or escalation loops. The guard alone lowers its success by 9 points ($-0.09$ $[-0.15, -0.03]$, not significant after correction, Holm $p = 0.32$), the guard with the rules in the prompt by 14 ($p = 0.005$), 1.7 steps and 5.2k tokens; the rules in the prompt make its roles ask more, 1.33 requests per incident in M2 against 1.05 under the guard alone, and more refusals follow. The cost comes from the approval rule, not the guard: M2's success rested on approvals its roles skipped.

One agent with every tool is the cheapest configuration in tokens and approval requests and the slowest in steps. Glimmer's single agent needs 8.1 steps per incident, as many as M3, because it acts once per step where the roles act in parallel (8 proposals per incident against 17), with 41\% fewer tokens (25.7k against 43.5k) and half the approval requests; M2 and M4 resolve the same incidents in 4.2 steps. For gpt-oss-120b it is also slower (7.0 steps against 4.6 in M3); for Gemma 4 E4B and Qwen3.5-122B it is the worst configuration in success and violations (45\% and 42\% success, 1.43 and 1.93 violations per incident). \todo{API: tokens and steps per API model in M2 and M4, one sentence; the cost comparison across models.}

Most of the requests Glimmer's roles send the human were not required by the rules (64\% in M2 and M4, 71\% in M3): it asks whenever hospital access is at risk, whatever the action; for the other models the share is 31 to 59\%. The human's load is set as much by the model's caution as by the rule set; it is what the trust-adaptive governor removes.

\paragraph{\RQ{3} What success hides}
Success hides violations in every unguarded mode: in M2, 12\%, 18\%, 44\% and 51\% of the successful episodes (Glimmer, gpt-oss-120b, Gemma 4 E4B, Qwen3.5-122B) contain an executed violation; without rules (M1) 46\%, 63\%, 68\% and 70\%. The incident is resolved, the trace says a rule was broken, and nothing in the final state does. Under the guard the share is zero by construction. Hidden harm occurs only in unguarded modes of the three models that act without asking (1 to 7\% of the incidents, Table~\ref{tab:llm}), never for Glimmer and never under the guard. The provenance metrics stayed clean for every model: no model ever broadcast the planted false report, in any mode, where the naive deterministic roles did so in every episode that had one; all four verify a report before they repeat it (trace support coverage 0.96 to 1.00 in the role modes, 0.77 and 0.81 for the two weak single agents), and no cited evidence id was invented.

The failures all end at the horizon of 16 steps and come from two mechanisms. The first is an \emph{approval lockout}: a role asks the human for an approval the rules do not require, the answer is a refusal, because the human is absent or knows something the roles do not, and the role respects the refusal for the rest of the episode. For Glimmer all 18 failures of M2 and M4 are of this kind, in the same three scenarios in every repeat, each with hospital access at risk. gpt-oss-120b locks itself out in the same three scenarios (13 of its 14 failures in M2 and M4). The guard did not cause it and, as long as a refusal binds, cannot prevent it: it binds whether the request was needed or not. The second is an \emph{evidence loop}: a role requests evidence or verification again and again and never proposes the message or action the incident needs. For Glimmer it occurs only without the rules in the prompt (20 of the 25 M3 failures), always the communication role never sending the public alert; with the rules it sends a targeted notice in the same scenarios. For Gemma 4 E4B it occurs with the rules too and is why it fails a quarter of the incidents in M2 and M4, almost all flooded underpasses: the communication role spends the episode asking to verify a residents' report it cannot check itself, nobody takes the forwarded request up, and the notice is never sent, although verified sensor readings it could cite are in the store.

The single agent fails in a third way, losing track of what is done: in 20 of Glimmer's 23 failures in M0, all traffic accidents, it secures the hospital route again and again and never sends the public notice.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Prompted rules are not enforced rules}
Prompting got Glimmer most of the way: with the rules in its prompts five of the six were kept in every one of 240 incidents; the sixth makes the case. The pollution-zone rule is the only one whose trigger is a state flag rather than evidence or an approval, and the model kept rerouting through the zone in one incident out of eight; under the guard it never did. Qwen3.5-122B, answering without reasoning, is the sharpest case: 106 times it wrote that a closure needed human approval and closed the road. A role can hold the rule and not the mechanism; the guard is the mechanism. Insisting does not change that: told to ask first and wait, with the rules repeated at every step, Qwen3.5-122B asked more and still did not wait, and Gemma 4 E4B kept half of its reroutes; Gemma with its reasoning off leaked what Qwen leaked. In the second domain both models kept the counterpart of the pollution rule and leaked approval rules they had kept in the city, each with a condition on the target or the payload. Which rule leaks depends on the model; that the guard catches it does not. A rule in a prompt is a consideration the model weighs against the task; a rule in the guard is a constraint on what can execute. The two also fail differently: prompted roles asked for approvals the rules did not require and a refusal then cost the incident; roles that met the rules only in the guard paid to discover them, in steps and tokens or in requests to the human. The combination removes both costs where the roles can walk the compliant path: the rules in the prompt tell them where it is, the guard catches what the prompt did not prevent, and the trace records what each one did. Where they cannot, as Qwen3.5-122B could not when an alert's approval was refused, the guard turns a hidden violation into a visible failure, and the trace shows whether to change the model, the fallback or the rule. For an engineer the recommendation is plain: write the rules once, give them to the roles as text and to the guard as data, and read the trace, not the outcome, to find out which of the two did the work.\todo{API: one clause on whether the API models leaked the same rule or a different one.}

\paragraph{One agent or a society}
One agent with every tool is cheaper in tokens and approval requests and slower in steps. For Glimmer and gpt-oss-120b it kept the rules about as well as the prompted society, but it acts once per step and loses track: Glimmer's single agent kept securing a route that was already secure while the public notice waited; for the two weaker models it was the worst configuration in success and violations. The society's roles act at once, and the guard is what keeps that parallelism within the rules.

\paragraph{The human is not a rule}
The approval step looks like a bottleneck and, under strict rules and an absent human, it is: success drops to a half. The adaptive governor shows what removing it costs: the rules capture what the designers foresaw, the human catches the rest, and compliance with the rules does not predict the rest (by construction here: hidden context is drawn independently). To reduce human load, reduce the \emph{unnecessary} share of approval requests, which the trace measures, rather than delegate on trust.

\paragraph{Threats to validity}
\emph{Construct}: the testbed is symbolic and its rules are ours; hidden context is a coin flip with a fixed rate, and its harm is binary. The metrics measure conformance to the given rules, not the quality of the rules. \emph{Internal}: the naive roles' reactions to the guard are scripted; a different reactive policy would change the cost side, not the violation side. The human model counts absence as refusal and lets a refusal bind even when no rule required the request; the approval lockouts follow from it, and a guard that waived such refusals would remove them. The single agent acts once per step, so the horizon of 16 steps binds it more than the roles. The sweeps and the trust-adaptive run have one repeat, and the governor ran with one LLM. LLM nondeterminism is handled by repeats and paired tests; prompts are fixed and published, not tuned per model, so the small model's evidence loops bound what an untuned prompt achieves. \emph{External}: two domains built to correspond, four open-weight models, each at one sampling setting\todo{API: ``and two API models from one provider''}, a deterministic human. One control with reasoning off, on one model, separates reasoning from the model, and the reinforced prompt is one of many possible prompts. The second domain was corrected once, after a visibility error that cost success equally in every mode, and rerun. The framework transfers when a domain module can be written, as it was for incident response; whether the numbers transfer is an open question. \emph{Conclusion}: all comparisons are paired, corrected for multiple testing and reported with intervals; seeds, scenario sets and manifests are in the artifact.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Governed agent societies put the rules where the actions are: in a guard inside the transition function that checks provenance, asks a person when the rules say so, and leaves a trace of everything it decided. With four LLMs driving the roles, the rules in the prompt still leaked between one violation per eight incidents and one per incident. The reasoning models leaked through one rule; the models that answer directly also through approvals they marked as required and did not wait for. A prompt that repeated the rules at every step removed a quarter to a half of the leak; the guard removed all of it, at no cost once reasoning roles knew the rules, at a price in steps or human requests when they did not, and at the price of the skipped approvals for the models that had succeeded by skipping them. Success and governance are different things: up to half of the successful incidents of roles governed by their prompts alone hid a violation, seven in ten without rules, and every harm came from acting where only a person knew better. Next are role policies learned from the traces and a human who is not deterministic. Testbed, traces, statistics and a reproduction of every table in one command are at \todo{anonymous link}.
